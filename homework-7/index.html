The conflict

Not every green solution looks green.

Hydropower generates electricity without burning fossil fuels, but dams transform rivers. Salmon can suffer from delayed migration, warmer reservoir water and changed habitat. The four lower Snake River dams also support regional navigation and reliable power. [2][7]

Rather than demolishing the dams, what if we paid significantly more to repair the habitat around them?

Important: This is a proposed policy, not a law. Conservation investment is already happening; the controversial choice is whether new money can achieve enough additional ecological benefit without dam removal.
The propositionClassroom ballot initiative

Title · 6 of 12 words

Keep the Dams. Restore Salmon Habitat.

Ballot question · 66 of 75 words

Should Congress require the Bonneville Power Administration to spend an additional $50 million annually for ten years on Snake River salmon habitat restoration, funded through wholesale electricity rates, while retaining the four lower Snake River dams in operation? Funds would target cold-water refuges, fish-passage barriers, stream restoration and independently measured outcomes. Existing fish-protection laws would remain in force; Congress would review the program after ten years.

The ballot is recording responses. As of October 18, 2026, 12 anonymous ballots have been received. You can still read the proposal and cast a vote while the poll remains open.

The strongest arguments, in both directions

Would this bargain actually work?

01

What's the Deal?

The lower Snake River's four federal dams provide hydropower and navigation, but dams and altered water conditions also make salmon survival harder. This proposal would retain Ice Harbor, Lower Monumental, Little Goose and Lower Granite dams while investing an additional $50 million per year for ten years ($500 million total) in Snake River Basin habitat. The Bonneville Power Administration would recover the extra spending through wholesale electricity rates, potentially increasing costs for utilities and customers. Tribes, fisheries, and salmon-dependent ecosystems could benefit from cooler tributaries, reopened spawning habitat and improved juvenile survival; ratepayers would pay. The program would be on top of current fish-and-wildlife spending, not a relabeling of existing budgets. It would not eliminate the dams' migration barriers or guarantee salmon recovery.

02

The Best Case for YES

Keep a major source of dispatchable, low-carbon electricity while repairing habitat salmon can actually use. Habitat restoration is not speculative technology: NOAA reported in 2026 that a $4.2 million effort to replace four Snake Basin road culverts is expected to reopen nearly 25 miles of habitat. Expanding tested interventions—barrier removal, riparian shade, cold-water connections, floodplain restoration and fish screens—could improve conditions without major disruption to power and grain transportation. A decade-long, independently evaluated investment would reveal which interventions work. BPA already administers a fish-and-wildlife program, so this uses an existing delivery institution. The bargain is to pay more for power to improve fish habitat while retaining the benefits of the dams.

03

The Best Case for NO

This plan may buy relatively small improvements while leaving the biggest obstacles intact. Salmon must still migrate through multiple reservoirs and dams; warmer water, predators and ocean conditions also threaten them. NOAA's basin-wide rebuilding report includes dam breaching among the actions most likely to help rebuild healthy, harvestable stocks. BPA already spends hundreds of millions annually on fish-and-wildlife mitigation across the Columbia Basin. Adding another $500 million over ten years could divert customers' money from more effective recovery options. Low-income households may be least able to absorb higher electricity bills, even if the charge begins at the wholesale level. A promise to preserve the dams could also delay necessary decisions and ignore tribal and fishing interests that favor removal.

04

Where Is the Body Buried?

Habitat gains are not the same as more returning adult salmon. Restoring upstream streams may have limited impact if migration through the dams and ocean survival remain the binding constraints. The $50 million yearly amount is a proposed policy choice, not an independently costed recovery package; it might be insufficient or wasteful. Additional ratepayer costs would also be unevenly passed along. This program needs independent benchmarks and a real ability to change course if fish survival fails to improve.

05

Why Isn't This Already Happening?

Partly, it is: BPA already funds extensive habitat projects through its existing fish-and-wildlife program, paid through wholesale power rates. The missing deal is a legislated additional, dedicated ten-year commitment with independently published results and explicit preservation of the four dams. Ratepayers have reason to resist new costs; dam-removal advocates can argue that paying to keep barriers is counterproductive. Benefits are dispersed across fisheries, ecosystems and future generations, while costs are concentrated in power bills. Congress controls the federal legal framework, and federal, tribal and state agencies do not agree on the best recovery strategy. Political conflict and uncertain biology make a bargain difficult even when the tools already exist.

The most interesting condition

One voter supported the general direction only if the ten-year cost dropped to $100 million rather than $500 million. Their objection was to the size of the proposed expenditure, not an explicit rejection of restoring salmon habitat.

Reflection: what this exercise showed me

The results challenged a simple yes-or-no view of environmental policy. Eleven voters accepted the proposal as written, but one supported it only with a significantly lower price tag. That suggests that even when people agree with an environmental goal, the amount of money being spent can determine whether a deal is acceptable. The conditional vote matters because it exposes a potential negotiation: how much restoration benefit would a smaller investment deliver, and what would be lost by spending less? This poll does not settle that question, but it shows why specifying who pays and how much is essential to an environmental bargain.

Sources & assumptions

  1. NOAA Fisheries (April 2026), Restoring Cold-Water Pathways for Idaho's Salmon and Steelhead — decline in salmon runs; $4.2M culvert project; nearly 25 miles of reopened habitat.
  2. U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Lower Snake River Dams — dam names, federal operation, hydropower and navigation.
  3. Bonneville Power Administration, Fish & Wildlife Program — existing program, institutions, habitat actions and limitations.
  4. Bonneville Power Administration, Funding and Ensuring Results — independent review and recovery of existing mitigation costs through electricity rates.
  5. Northwest Power and Conservation Council, 2024 Report on BPA Fish and Wildlife Expenditures — FY2024 direct program spending of $270.8M; not the same as total fish-related costs.
  6. NOAA (2022 report; 2025 policy update), Rebuilding Salmon and Steelhead in the Columbia River Basin — habitat restoration and dam breaching both among recovery actions; 2025 federal agreement withdrawal.
  7. NOAA Fisheries, How Dams Affect Water and Habitat on the West Coast — habitat, flow, temperature and migration mechanisms.

Methods and uncertainty. $50 million/year for ten years ($500 million total) is the author's proposed policy amount, not an official cost estimate or guarantee of species recovery. It is additional to existing BPA fish-and-wildlife spending. Wholesale rate recovery does not imply a predictable identical charge on each household. Proposed projects require prioritization with tribal and state co-managers; independently measured milestones should include stream miles reopened, seasonal temperatures, juvenile survival and adult returns, with important caveats about ocean conditions and detection lag. Nothing here waives Endangered Species Act or tribal treaty protections.
