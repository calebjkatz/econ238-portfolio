The denominator

A count is only half the story.

A death count describes the scale of loss. A population-adjusted rate relates that loss to the number of people who could be affected. If population grows while deaths stay constant, the rate falls—even though the number of lives lost has not changed.

Movement & exposure

Where we move changes what we face.

Selected hazard: hurricanes

Draft analytical framing: replace or revise the following with the final migration evidence and a 150–250 word interpretation.

For hurricanes, the question is whether moves within the United States are shifting people toward more exposed places. A growing coastal population does not establish that by itself. Growth can also reflect births, deaths, or international immigration. Census estimates report these components separately, so the relevant starting point is net domestic migration, not total population change. [3]

The planned comparison groups counties by a documented measure of hurricane exposure, then compares domestic migration rates over the same period. Rates matter here too: a large county can gain more residents without attracting a larger share of its starting population. Exposure should be measured independently of the migration outcome, with the geographic boundaries and observation years held consistent.

Even if more exposed counties gain domestic migrants, that would describe where people are moving, not why they move. Jobs, housing costs, family ties, and amenities are possible explanations that would need separate evidence. It would also leave open whether safer buildings, evacuation systems, or other adaptations offset some additional exposure. Until the migration and hazard data are joined and checked, no directional finding is claimed here.

The economics

Risk is shaped by choices—and constraints.

The IPCC’s SREX framework distinguishes the physical hazard, the people and assets exposed to it, and their vulnerability. These are different ways risk can change. [4]

Hazard
The physical event.
Exposure
Who and what is in its path.
Vulnerability
How susceptible they are to harm.

Economics asks what people can change, what those changes cost, and who has the means to act. Warnings are more useful when people have transport and somewhere safe to go. Stronger buildings require investment; reliable heating and cooling require equipment, energy, and the ability to pay.

Prices, insurance, building rules, and public infrastructure can change the incentives to protect a home or move away. Protection can also make an exposed place more attractive, so an adaptation may reduce vulnerability while encouraging additional exposure. That is a mechanism to test, not a result established by a national mortality chart.

Wealth and technology expand the options available, but benefits depend on access and institutions. The relevant tradeoff is not “safety or no safety.” It is which feasible protections deliver the greatest benefit, to whom, and at what opportunity cost.

A different measure

Temperature’s toll extends beyond the event count.

Burke et al. (2025), Understanding and Addressing Temperature Impacts on Mortality, studies mortality associated with temperatures above and below locally relevant minimum-risk temperatures. [5]

Do not combine these statistics

Two measures. Different questions.

NWS event fatalities

Recorded deaths assigned to weather-event categories under the selected dataset’s definitions.

What deaths were counted in those events?

Temperature-attributable mortality

Statistical estimates of deaths associated with temperatures relative to a minimum-mortality reference. These can include deaths coded to other causes.

How would mortality differ under the reference temperature?

Do not append the paper’s estimates to the NWS charts, add the totals together, or treat the difference as an undercount. Their definitions and methods differ. [1] [5]

Cold, heat, and local adaptation

In the largely temperate populations studied, estimated cold-attributable mortality exceeds heat-attributable mortality. The authors also find evidence of adaptation to local climate. Neither finding establishes that warming is beneficial everywhere or that adaptation eliminates temperature risk. [5]

From a promising technology to a feasible intervention

The paper reviews interventions involving indoor temperatures, behavior, and access to services, while highlighting gaps in causal evidence. A technology’s ability to change temperature is not proof that a particular program reduces deaths. [5]

The inference to challenge

But wait…

Fewer deaths would not, by themselves, prove that storms have become less dangerous.

If the completed charts show falling mortality, that pattern could be consistent with improved warnings, safer structures, different exposure, or changes in measurement—even alongside more severe hazards. A mortality trend does not isolate the contribution of each mechanism.

Separating those explanations requires comparable measures of event frequency and intensity, where people live, the protections available, and reporting practices. This caveat would not erase a verified decline in mortality; it would limit what that decline tells us about the weather itself.

The takeaway

Read the weather.
Read the world around it.

Managing environmental risk means understanding both the physical event and the conditions that turn exposure into harm. Counts, population-adjusted rates, migration, and temperature-attributable estimates answer different questions. Read together—with their limits intact—they can inform decisions about adaptation.

How the final comparisons will be constructed

Time: begin with 1940–1949 and use ten-year groups thereafter. Report the exact endpoint and number of observed years in any partial decade. No cutoff is implied by an empty chart.

Counts: divide each hazard’s total by observed years. Distinguish zeros from missing entries. Check definitions and reporting breaks before combining historical files.

Rates: divide the hazard total by the sum of annual population estimates, then multiply by one million. This is a pooled rate per million person-years, not an unweighted average of annual rates. It adjusts for population size, not age or hazard exposure.

Migration: use a documented domestic migration measure and consistent county boundaries, exposure classification, and time window. Net county gains do not by themselves identify migrants’ origins or prove why they moved.
