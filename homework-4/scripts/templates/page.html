Extreme Weather, Risk, and Adaptation / Homework 4

Risk Didn't Disappear.
Vulnerability Changed.

From the 1940s to the 2010s, annual U.S. deaths from tornadoes, floods, and hurricanes fell from about 1.91 to 0.61 per million people—even as Americans continued to live in exposed places.

The historical record

Fewer lives lost.
An uneven path.

The combined annual toll from these three reported hazards averaged 262.3 deaths in the 1940s and 195.1 in the 2010s. That is about 26% lower, but the decline is neither smooth nor shared equally by every hazard. Flood mortality in the 2010s exceeded its 1940s average, and large individual events can reshape a decade. [1]

That uneven record matters. Forecasting, warnings, stronger structures, transportation, emergency response, and learning can reduce harm. The chart records the outcomes; it cannot isolate how much each mechanism contributed.

What “Hurricane” means here. Current NWS documentation assigns hurricane/tropical-cyclone wind deaths to that event category; flooding, storm surge, and tornado deaths may appear in other categories. Historical classifications also need care. This column is not every death associated with every tropical cyclone. [1]

Across 1940–2025, the three series total 20,676 deaths, or approximately 240.42 per year: 8,094 tornado, 8,991 flood, and 3,591 hurricane deaths. The decade comparison above is the central evidence.

The denominator

More than twice as many people.
A much lower rate.

Average U.S. resident population grew from 137.7 million to 319.3 million between these two decades. Once that larger denominator is included, the decline in mortality is much larger than the drop in raw counts. [2]

Divide each decade’s combined average annual fatalities by its average population, then multiply by one million. The resulting statistic describes reported deaths relative to population. It is not a causal-model probability, an age-adjusted rate, or a personal risk estimate for someone living in a floodplain.

Movement & exposure / Flooding

Americans did not simply retreat from risk.

Flood exposure looks very different depending on the geographic unit. Qin and Voorheis (2026), using Census-linked administrative records and parcel-level flood maps for 1999–2023, show that the number of households in flood-prone places rose substantially. Under their expansive FEMA parcel definition, exposed households increased from roughly 11 million to more than 15 million. But that does not mean Americans are steadily migrating into flood risk. The share of households exposed stayed around 14.2–14.4 percent, and the researchers find that inflows and outflows are roughly balanced when exposure is measured at the parcel level. At the broader Census-block level, however, migration can look like movement toward floodplains. [3]

The economic lesson is that exposure is determined at a finer scale than regional population growth. Housing costs, amenities, insurance, infrastructure, building choice, and perceived risk can affect where households locate within the same growing region. This complicates the storm-mortality story. Lower population-adjusted weather mortality can coexist with continued flood exposure because exposure is only one part of risk; protective capital, forecasting, warnings, emergency response, behavior, and other forms of adaptation also influence outcomes.

Household growth is not the same as internal migration. The exposed-household count also reflects changes in population and household formation. Births and international immigration are distinct from moves within the United States. The increase from ~11 million to >15 million does not establish that migration added four million exposed households.

The economics of living with risk

Protection has a price.
So does moving.

Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability

A conceptual framework, not an estimated equation. Mortality is a realized outcome; it does not tell us which component changed. [5]

People may accept exposure in exchange for jobs, affordable housing, family ties, coastal access, or other amenities. Their choices depend on perceived risk and on the capital and technology available to protect them. A move within a growing region can change flood exposure without appearing in a county-level migration statistic.

Private measures—safer construction, insurance, equipment, and responding to forecasts—interact with public goods such as warnings, drainage, transport, and emergency response. Insurance subsidies may change incentives to occupy exposed places; inaccurate perceptions of rare events can also distort choices. These are mechanisms to investigate, not effects identified by the two mortality charts.

Reducing exposure may sometimes cost less than protecting an existing location, but relocation has costs too. Access to wealth, health care, reliable energy, cooling or heating, and adaptive infrastructure is unequal. The practical question is which feasible protection delivers the greatest benefit, who pays, and whose remaining risk is tolerated.

A different mortality lens

The definition changes the number.

Burke et al. (2025) analyze detailed data from 30 countries and estimate that ambient temperature accounts for approximately 5–12% of total deaths across countries in their sample. This is the NBER Working Paper 34313 version, not a direct weather-event tally. [4]

Do not compare as equivalent totals

These numbers are not measuring the same thing.

NWS weather fatalities

Event/hazard-based reported fatalities recorded in particular weather categories.

Temperature-attributable mortality

Statistical estimates based on how overall mortality changes with ambient temperature. Many affected deaths need not appear in a direct NWS heat or cold count.

How does mortality differ with temperature?

Different definitions and methods; not competing estimates of one quantity. Do not add these estimates to the NWS totals or infer an undercount from their difference. [1] [4]

Local adaptation is not immunity.

Cold causes more attributable mortality than heat in the contexts studied. Hotter places show somewhat lower risk at high temperatures but encounter those temperatures more often. Higher income within countries is not uniformly associated with lower temperature vulnerability, and the authors find limited rigorous causal evidence for many proposed interventions. [4]

Cooling access: a feasibility question.

Consider a targeted program that helps households obtain and operate cooling equipment. Assessing it requires more than its purchase price: electricity bills, maintenance, grid reliability, outreach, and actual use affect feasibility. Benefits should be evaluated against health outcomes, not simply units distributed. A fair comparison includes other uses of the same budget, such as housing improvements or access to care.

That is an economic evaluation framework, not a claim that a particular subsidy is proven cost-effective. Without a specified population, program cost, and credible effect estimate, a numerical benefit–cost ratio would be false precision.

The inference to challenge

But wait—does declining mortality prove adaptation?

No. The pattern is consistent with reduced vulnerability; the comparison is not a causal experiment.

Event frequency, severity, and location changed alongside settlement patterns, population composition, forecasts, reporting, and fatality classification. Rare catastrophic events can dominate an entire decade. The NWS hurricane column also does not capture every tropical-cyclone death.

Identifying adaptation’s contribution would require comparable hazard and exposure measures and evidence that separates specific protections from these other changes. The data establish lower population-adjusted mortality in the three reported categories. They do not establish how much adaptation caused. This qualifies the conclusion without erasing the decline.

The takeaway

Living with risk.
Not eliminating it.

The United States did not eliminate extreme-weather risk. From the 1940s to the 2010s, average annual mortality from tornadoes, floods, and hurricanes fell from about 1.91 deaths per million residents to 0.61. Meanwhile, detailed flood data show continued exposure and no clear parcel-level net migration away from flood risk.

Economics helps reconcile those facts. Risk depends on the physical hazard, where people live, what they value, the technology and capital protecting them, and how they respond. The falling mortality rate is consistent with changing vulnerability—not proof that the hazard disappeared, not a causal estimate of adaptation, and not a promise that future protection will be free or automatic.

Calculations, definitions, and limits

Raw averages: annual reported fatalities summed within each period and divided by ten years for complete decades or six years for 2020–25. Combined averages sum the three categories. Across the full 86-year period: 20,676 ÷ 86 ≈ 240.42 deaths/year. The partial-period hurricane and combined averages retain the supplied two-decimal rounding.

Population adjustment: combined average annual deaths ÷ average decade population × 1,000,000. The endpoint calculation is 262.3 ÷ 137,686,400 × 1,000,000 = 1.905054 and 195.1 ÷ 319,333,559 × 1,000,000 = 0.610960. The decline is (1 − 0.610960 ÷ 1.905054) × 100 ≈ 67.93%, calculated before rounding. CSV display rates are the supplied three-decimal values.

Population provenance: the supplied decade averages are the denominator for all published rates. The linked archived historical file produces different averages for several decades, so the rates are reproducible from the published CSV but not independently reconciled to a single annual Census extract. No supplied value has been replaced.

Scope: this is a descriptive U.S. resident-population normalization of the selected NWS reported hazard series. NWS documentation describes reporting across states and certain territories; the table is used as supplied, not reconstructed as a strictly 50-state matched-geography or individual probability estimate. Population adjustment does not control for age, event intensity, exposure, or changes in reporting.

Migration: the evidence concerns households, not a count of individual people. The FEMA parcel definition includes A, V, or 500-year floodplains. Counts, shares, and net migration are distinct. Approximate endpoints and a share range do not supply an exact annual time series. The study’s underlying confidential administrative microdata are not reproduced here.

Reproducibility: mortality CSV, population CSV, flood summary CSV, and unrounded calculations. The repository includes scripts to rebuild the vector charts, tables, page, and print poster from these inputs.
