# Can America Still Build? Trust, Regulation, and the Environmental Economy

*Caleb Katz · Econ 238 · Week Three*

This week's discussion looks at why the United States has so much difficulty building major infrastructure, including projects needed for a cleaner energy economy. The conversation focuses on institutional trust, environmental review, America's legal and regulatory system, and the tradeoff between protecting communities and allowing projects to be built more quickly.

## Synthesis

The biggest thing I took away from these readings is that America's infrastructure problem cannot be blamed on one law like NEPA. Potter, Doomberg, Howard, and Wang are describing different parts of a larger problem. Potter shows how NEPA creates incentives for agencies to spend huge amounts of time making documents that are difficult to challenge in court. Doomberg takes this further and argues that litigation itself has become deeply embedded in American institutions. Howard focuses more on bureaucracy and argues that we need to replace strict procedural rules with responsibility and judgment. Wang compares this American “lawyerly society” with China's “engineering state,” where the focus is much more on actually getting things built. The problem is that neither extreme seems desirable. China's system can build incredibly quickly, but the same system can ignore individual rights and public concerns. America's system protects those rights, but can make building almost anything extremely difficult.

One reform I think would help is giving one agency clear authority over a project and making that agency responsible for reaching a decision within a reasonable timeline. This would change the incentives of government employees. Right now, an employee may be better off spending six more months documenting everything than approving something and risking a lawsuit. If their responsibility instead became making a well-supported decision within a deadline, there would be more incentive to focus on the actual environmental tradeoffs rather than creating the longest possible document.

The immediate benefit would be faster permitting, but the effects would go further. Faster and more predictable approvals could reduce uncertainty and make companies more willing to invest in transmission lines, nuclear power, renewable generation, and other infrastructure needed for a cleaner energy system. However, there is a real cost. Moving toward more discretion means trusting the people making these decisions, and communities could lose some ability to challenge projects that genuinely hurt them.

This became more important to me during my conversation. We talked about putting more engineers and scientists into government decision-making, but my conversation partner was hesitant to simply give experts more power. That made me realize that technical knowledge and public trust are not the same thing. Someone might be qualified to determine whether something is safe without the public trusting that person to decide what risks are acceptable.

I still think America needs to become more willing and able to build, but reform cannot just mean removing rules. The unresolved question for me is how much discretion we can give decision-makers while still creating enough accountability that the public trusts the decisions they make.
