The central distinction

Recognition is not control.

Land acknowledgments can preserve memory and start conversations. But they do not, by themselves, change who can use land, raise money against it, develop it, conserve it, or receive its income.

That is the question at the heart of the class audio discussion: What would make environmental justice more than a statement?

A well-intentioned policy is not necessarily a policy that changes the incentives people face.

Three cases, three lessons

What does action look like?

Rochester, New York

When an acknowledgment opens a door

The University of Rochester International Theatre Program pairs its acknowledgment with free admission to public performances for members of the Seneca Nation and Tonawanda Seneca Nation. This is modest compared with land restitution, but unlike a statement alone, it offers an identifiable benefit. [1]

The test: Does the gesture lead to a lasting relationship, or is it where the relationship ends?

Vancouver, Canada

When land becomes an economic choice

At Sen̓áḵw, the Squamish Nation is developing housing on land returned in 2003. The project combines housing, financing, employment opportunities, and tribal control over long-term decisions. Phase One is planned for 1,409 homes; later phases have a different ownership structure. The lesson is not that development is always preferable to preservation. It is that meaningful land rights let the Nation make that choice. [2]

The tradeoff: More economic opportunity requires decisions about building, financing, access, and how benefits are distributed.

National parks, United States

When stewardship meets public access

Should national parks be returned to the tribes whose ancestors were displaced? Full transfers raise difficult issues of ownership claims, conservation obligations, fee setting, and access. A narrower possibility already exists: in 2024 the National Park Service signed co-stewardship agreements with the Miccosukee Tribe for Everglades and Biscayne national parks. [3]

The test: Does shared authority create demonstrably better outcomes—or merely an advisory role with a new name?

An experiment, not a leap of faith

My proposed park-management test

Rather than transferring every national park immediately, begin with a small set of willing tribal governments and parks. Give participating tribes meaningful authority, negotiated revenue rights, and a defined role in conservation. Compare their results over ten years with suitably matched parks under conventional management.

Ecological healthHabitat condition, wildlife indicators, erosion, and restoration results
Economic opportunityTribal income, local employment, maintenance costs, tourism services
Public valueAccess, affordability, visitor experience, and cultural stewardship

This is a proposed evaluation design, not an existing experiment or a claim that one governance model will necessarily outperform another.

Questions worth taking away

What would change your mind?

  • If a land acknowledgment changes no legal rights, can it still be valuable?
  • When cleaner environments make property more expensive, who actually receives the benefit?
  • Would you support tribal management of a park if admission became more expensive?
  • If a tribe could choose to close a park to protect it, how much control did you really mean to give back?
  • What would you measure in ten years to know that justice—and conservation—improved?

The argument: I do not support an immediate blanket transfer of all national parks. I do support seriously testing co-management and potentially broader land rights. The goal is measurable improvement in people's lives, not a claim that one institution is automatically virtuous.
