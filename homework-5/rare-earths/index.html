Rare earths · U.S. vs. China

Mine ≠ Magnet

The United States is mining rare earths again. So why is the supply chain still considered vulnerable?

Because digging up ore is only the first step. The harder economic problem is rebuilding separation, metal refining, alloying, and magnet manufacturing after decades of those capabilities moving overseas.

01 / The number that changed my question

The U.S. has ore.
China has the chain.

USGS estimates that the United States produced 51,000 metric tons of rare-earth-oxide-equivalent mineral concentrate in 2025—about 13% of the rounded world total. China produced 270,000 tons, about 69%. Yet USGS reports China accounted for roughly 87% of all rare-earth processing in 2024. [1][2]

Economic takeaway: “Produce more rare earths in America” can mean at least four different industrial policies. More mining alone can still leave the value-added separation, refining, and magnet stages concentrated elsewhere.

02 / How did this happen?

The U.S. did not run out
of rare earths.

The decline was a mix of competition, environmental and regulatory problems, manufacturing migration, technical know-how moving with industry, and later a failed attempt to restart at scale.

1965–85

Mountain Pass era

The United States was the dominant global producer, and U.S. industry performed all major stages of the rare-earth supply chain. [3]

Mid-1980s–90s

China scales up

Chinese production rose rapidly. USGS later pointed to lower labor and regulatory costs, favorable deposits, growth of Asian manufacturing, and technology/expertise shifting toward Asia. [4]

1998

Separation shuts down

Mountain Pass's separation plant closed after regulatory problems involving its wastewater pipeline. [3]

2002–05

Mine and magnet capacity fade

Mountain Pass mining suspended in 2002. GAO recorded U.S. NdFeB magnet facilities closing or moving equipment to China in the following years. [3]

2015

Molycorp fails

A major restart effort ended in bankruptcy, illustrating that owning a deposit does not guarantee an economically viable integrated processing business. [5]

2023–26

Midstream returns

MP Materials began separated NdPr production at Mountain Pass, followed by metal and early magnet production in Texas. Other public and private projects are now targeting separation, heavy rare earths, recycling, and magnets. [6][7]

03 / The lanthanides are not interchangeable

Which elements actually
matter for magnets?

Click the elements below. One reason rare-earth processing is difficult is that neighboring lanthanides have very similar chemistry, yet the market values their individual properties very differently.

Why separation is the bottleneck

Conventional solvent extraction can require long trains of mixer-settlers because rare earths are chemically similar. DOE notes that separating Nd from Pr alone can require about 30 mixer-settlers in some processes, while broader separation trains can involve hundreds. These processes consume acids, caustic, water, and generate wastewater that must be managed. [5]

04 / What is actually coming back?

Separate operating capacity
from announced capacity.

“The U.S. is rebuilding rare earths” is true, but projects are at very different stages. This exhibit keeps operating output separate from construction, pilot demonstrations, and award negotiations.

OPERATING / RAMPING

Mountain Pass + Independence

MP Materials reported 2,599 t of NdPr oxide production in 2025 and began producing finished NdFeB magnets on commercial equipment. In Q2 2026 it reported 840 t of NdPr production and continued magnet qualification. These are company-reported operating figures. [6][7]

UNDER CONSTRUCTION

10X · Northlake, Texas

MP broke ground in 2026 on a larger magnet campus. The company says the combined platform is intended to reach about 10,000 t/yr of NdFeB magnet capacity. That is planned capacity, not current output. [8]

PILOT / PRE-COMMERCIAL

USA Rare Earth · Oklahoma

DOE selected a pilot-scale continuous ion-exchange separation project intended to demonstrate an alternative to incumbent solvent-extraction technology. Selection and pilot scale are not the same as sustained commercial production. [9]

SELECTED FOR AWARD NEGOTIATIONS

Waste-to-rare-earth demonstrations

In June 2026 DOE announced $134 million for Colorado School of Mines and Phoenix Tailings teams to demonstrate recovery and refining from red mud, industrial waste, and other unconventional feedstocks. Selection is explicitly not a final award or proof of commercial viability. [10]

05 / The part I did not expect

Promethium is a lanthanide—but not the supply-chain story I thought it was.

Promethium (Pm) sits between neodymium and samarium on the periodic table, but DOE notes that it has no stable isotopes. It is radioactive and produced in specialized nuclear contexts rather than mined and separated as a normal commercial magnet feedstock. For permanent magnets, the economically important lanthanides are instead elements such as Nd, Pr, Sm, Dy, and Tb. [11]

06 / Environmental economics

Domestic does not
automatically mean clean.

Rebuilding the chain changes where environmental costs, industrial jobs, supply risk, and public spending occur. The relevant comparison is not “mining versus no mining,” but one supply chain versus another.

Supply resilience

More domestic and allied capacity reduces dependence on a highly concentrated processor and can lower exposure to export controls. China tightened controls on several rare earths in 2025, including samarium, terbium, and dysprosium products. [1]

Environmental cost

Separation can consume large amounts of acid, caustic, water, and energy. Wastewater and, for some ores, uranium or thorium add treatment and disposal costs. Stronger environmental standards can raise private production costs while reducing external environmental damage. [5][12]

Scale economics

Heavy rare earth separation can be uneconomic at small volumes. A new domestic plant needs enough throughput and buyers to spread fixed costs—one reason offtake agreements and public support matter. [5]

Industrial policy

Current U.S. strategy uses grants, loans, stockpiling, R&D, and long-term purchasing commitments to create demand certainty. The benefit is resilience; the tradeoff is public capital and the risk of supporting projects that may not become cost-competitive.

My takeaway: I originally thought the key question was whether the United States could mine more lanthanides. The bigger constraint is whether a full chain can survive economically—from mixed concentrate to separated oxides to metals and magnets—while meeting U.S. environmental standards and competing with an industry China has built at enormous scale.

Methods and caveats

The 2025 mining shares are calculated from USGS's rounded world total, so percentages are approximate. The 87% processing share is a 2024 USGS estimate. The 92% magnet-manufacturing figure is a DOE benchmark for 2020 and is intentionally labeled historical; it is not presented as a 2026 estimate. MP Materials production and project-capacity figures come from the company and are identified as such. DOE project selections do not guarantee final awards or commercial success. “Rare earths” commonly includes the 15 lanthanides plus yttrium and scandium; this exhibit emphasizes lanthanides relevant to permanent magnets.
